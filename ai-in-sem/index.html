The Review

Introduction

1 in 7 people
report using AI chatbots instead of contacting a GP
1 in 5
decided against professional advice on that basis

Sport & Exercise Medicine (SEM) is unusually exposed because its interventions span weeks or months without direct clinician supervision. This review investigated the use of AI in SEM and adjacent fields to:

  • Appraise the evidence base and regulatory landscape
  • Evaluate opportunities and risks
  • Deliver priorities for responsible development, validation & implementation
Methods

Search strategy & included literature

Search strategy

A structured literature search and analysis was conducted using Aletheia and Oghma — search, evaluation, and analytic engines built on a bespoke research platform under development by the group (Symposion, Aurora Performance, UK). This included:

  • Conventional biomedical science databases
  • Focused search of major organisations
  • Citation searching & snowballing
Included literature

Peer-reviewed articles, grey literature, frameworks & standards, legal & regulatory documents, real-world cases.

Results

139 sources, four domains of application

Sources spanned biomedicine (SEM, orthopaedics, allied MSK), informatics (digital health, software engineering, AI), and regulatory literature (legal & governance standards).

Key findings

  • Poor evidence: across all contexts, especially in athletes, sport, and nutrition
  • AI chatbots: widespread use (1 in 7 of the public), problematic in ~50% of responses
  • Acceptability: evidence of engagement decline if unsupervised
  • Governance: mostly voluntary, with little unified regulatory oversight
  • Consequence: higher in unsupervised or decision-support tools
  • Priorities: validation, stakeholder-led design, reporting standards

Discussion

Weighing the evidence

There is evidence of potential benefits of AI in SEM — e.g. clinical decision support, supported self-management, individualised care, health data science, data collection, and service improvement & delivery.

We classify AI tools according to the end user: Provider (e.g. clinician); Service User (e.g. patient); Non-clinical (e.g. researcher).

Where risk is highest
  • AI tools requiring unsupervised patient operation
  • Tools relied on for clinical decision-making
  • Where the consequence of error is serious or irreversible
  • Regulation is almost entirely voluntary; oversight is fragmented
Where implementation is defensible
  • Where scale is the benefit and error is recoverable
  • Supported self-management and individualised care
  • Health data science, data collection, service improvement
"Implementation is most defensible where scale is the benefit and error is recoverable."

Recommended priorities for responsible design & implementation

  • Declared purpose prospectively, which determines regulatory class
  • Validation of AI tool performance in the target population
  • Safety demonstration with deliberate, specific, standardised testing
  • Professional body involvement in guidance for use and regulation
  • Stakeholder involvement in tool development, research, and governance
